# Interview Frameworks — The Grilling's DNA

> Where the Socratic interview questions come from, why each matters, and how to push harder when the answer is soft.

This reference deepens Mode A. The SKILL.md has the eight questions. This document has the methodology behind each, the frameworks they draw from, and the follow-up playbook when the first answer isn't enough.

---

## 1. The Problem — "Describe the bad day"

**Framework:** Jobs-to-be-Done (Clayton Christensen, Harvard Business School)

People don't buy products. They hire them to do a job. The job is the pain — the thing they're trying to accomplish that their current tools fail at.

**What you're listening for:**

- A specific person (not "users" or "the market")
- A specific moment (not "generally, people struggle with...")
- Emotional weight (frustration, wasted time, fear, embarrassment)

**Push-back playbook:**

- "That sounds like a market opportunity, not a pain. Who hurts?" → forces specificity
- "How much does this cost them today — in time, money, or sanity?" → forces quantification
- "If you could follow them through their worst day, what would you see?" → forces narrative

**Source:** Christensen, C. (2003). _The Innovator's Solution_. Harvard Business School Press. Also: Klement, A. (2018). _When Coffee and Kale Compete_. NYC Publishing.

## 2. The Current Solution — "What's broken?"

**Framework:** The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick)

You learn more from how people currently solve the problem than from what they say they want. Current behavior is evidence; stated preferences are noise.

**What you're listening for:**

- What people actually DO today (not what they wish they could do)
- Workarounds — the uglier the workaround, the bigger the pain
- Money already being spent on bad solutions (proves willingness to pay)

**Push-back playbook:**

- "You said they use Excel for this. How many hours a week?" → quantifies the pain
- "Have they tried other tools? Why did those fail?" → reveals what matters
- "If they're not actively looking for a solution, is this really painful enough?" → challenges premise

**Source:** Fitzpatrick, R. (2013). _The Mom Test: How to talk to customers and learn if your business is a good idea when everyone is lying to you_. Robfitz Ltd.

## 3. Your Solution — "What happens when they use it?"

**Framework:** 10x Better Test (Peter Thiel / Y Combinator)

A new product must be 10x better than the alternative on the dimension that matters most. Not 2x, not "slightly better UX" — 10x. Otherwise switching costs kill adoption.

**What you're listening for:**

- A concrete user experience, not a feature list
- The ONE dimension where it's dramatically better (speed, cost, accuracy, convenience)
- Simplicity — if the explanation takes more than 2 sentences, it's too complex

**Push-back playbook:**

- "You listed six features. Which ONE would make someone switch?" → forces prioritization
- "If you could only build one thing, what would it be?" → reveals the core value
- "Explain it to me like I'm your customer, not your investor" → kills jargon

**Source:** Thiel, P. (2014). _Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future_. Crown Business. Also: Y Combinator's startup evaluation criteria (publicly documented in YC's library).

## 4. Why You — "What do you see that others don't?"

**Framework:** Founder-Market Fit (Marc Andreessen / a16z)

The best founders have an unfair insight — something they know from lived experience that others would need years to learn. This isn't about credentials; it's about perspective.

**What you're listening for:**

- Personal connection to the problem (lived it, watched someone suffer from it)
- Domain expertise that can't be hired (years in the industry, technical depth, cultural insight)
- Obsession — will they still work on this when it's hard and boring?

**Push-back playbook:**

- "You said you have 10 years of experience. What did year 8 teach you that year 2 didn't?" → tests depth
- "If Google started working on this tomorrow with 50 engineers, what would you still do better?" → tests uniqueness
- "What's the thing about this market that pisses you off personally?" → reveals obsession

**Source:** Andreessen, M. (2007). "Product/Market Fit." _pmarchive.com_. Also: Dalton Caldwell & Michael Seibel on founder-market fit in YC interviews.

## 5. Why Now — "What changed?"

**Framework:** Sequoia Capital's "Why Now?" (from their pitch template)

Every great company has a "why now" — a change in the world that makes this the right moment. Without it, the idea is either too early (the world isn't ready) or too late (someone already won).

**What you're listening for:**

- A specific change: technology breakthrough, regulatory shift, behavior change, cost collapse
- Timing evidence: "This wasn't possible until GPT-4 / until GDPR / until COVID changed behavior"
- Window awareness: "We have 18-24 months before the big players notice"

**Push-back playbook:**

- "This problem existed five years ago. Why didn't someone build this then?" → forces timing insight
- "What if you're two years too early? How would you know?" → tests awareness
- "What specific event or trend gave you the signal to start NOW?" → tests specificity

**Source:** Sequoia Capital. "Writing a Business Plan." _sequoiacap.com_ (publicly available). Also: Bill Gross, "The single biggest reason why startups succeed" (TED, 2015) — timing as the #1 predictor of startup success.

## 6. The Moat — "What stops copies?"

**Framework:** Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers

Helmer identified seven types of durable competitive advantage. Only structural moats survive — a head start is not a moat.

The seven powers:

1. **Scale economies** — unit costs fall as you grow (AWS, Costco)
2. **Network effects** — product gets better with more users (Facebook, Airbnb)
3. **Counter-positioning** — incumbent can't copy you without cannibalizing their own business (Netflix vs. Blockbuster)
4. **Switching costs** — painful to leave once you're in (Salesforce, Epic)
5. **Branding** — earned trust that commands premium (Apple, Patagonia)
6. **Cornered resource** — exclusive access to talent, data, or IP (Google's talent density)
7. **Process power** — organizational know-how that takes years to build (Toyota, TSMC)

**What you're listening for:**

- Which specific power(s) the company will build
- Whether the moat is structural (grows with the business) or temporal (just a head start)
- Data moats — does usage generate data that improves the product and can't be replicated?

**Push-back playbook:**

- "You said 'first mover advantage.' That's a head start, not a moat. What makes it permanent?" → forces structural thinking
- "If someone raised $50M to copy your product, what couldn't they replicate?" → tests defensibility
- "Which of Helmer's 7 Powers does your company build?" → forces framework rigor

**Source:** Helmer, H. (2016). _7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy_. Hamilton Helmer.

## 7. The Contrarian Truth — "What do you believe that others don't?"

**Framework:** Peter Thiel's Contrarian Question

"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" This is the intellectual foundation of every 0-to-1 company. The answer must be both contrarian AND correct — just being different isn't enough.

**What you're listening for:**

- A belief that would make most industry insiders uncomfortable
- Evidence that the belief is correct (not just a hunch)
- An explanation of WHY the conventional view is wrong (mechanism, not opinion)

**Push-back playbook:**

- "That sounds like the consensus view. What's YOUR take that's different?" → pushes past safe answers
- "If you're right about this, what else must be true?" → tests internal consistency
- "What would convince you that you're wrong?" → tests intellectual honesty

**Source:** Thiel, P. (2014). _Zero to One_. Also: Thiel's CS183 Stanford lectures (publicly available as Blake Masters' notes).

## 8. The North Star — "What does the world look like?"

**Framework:** Definite Optimism (Peter Thiel) + Insurgent Mission (Bain & Company)

The best visions are definite — they describe a specific future, not a vague hope. And the best founders have an insurgent mission: they're fighting for something, not just building something.

**What you're listening for:**

- A specific picture of the future (not "we'll be the leading platform for...")
- Impact on actual humans (not revenue targets or market share)
- Something worth fighting for over 10 years — because that's how long it takes

**Push-back playbook:**

- "That sounds like a revenue target. What changes for the people you serve?" → redirects to impact
- "If you succeed, what headlines get written?" → forces vivid specificity
- "Would you still want this future if you weren't the one building it?" → tests mission vs. ego

**Source:** Thiel, P. (2014). _Zero to One_. Zook, C. & Allen, J. (2016). _The Founder's Mentality_. Bain & Company / Harvard Business Review Press.
